\section{Abstract}
Colonial-era isiXhosa newspapers preserve African intellectual debate, but their pages, issues and language views are not interchangeable evidence. We test how those units affect NLP measurements. A matched $2\times2$ tokenizer study in bilingual \emph{Imvo} separates allocation between language views from page choice: word-like allocation lowers English-view segmentation cost but raises isiXhosa-view cost, without uniform predictive gains. Separately, ArchiveLens compares four estimands for 1885-to-1888 character-pattern changes. Raw sign reversals are ordinary under whole-issue permutations, yet 29 \emph{Imvo} maximum estimator gaps survive Holm correction, all attained by equal-page versus character-mass weighting. Exploratory page-position analysis accounts for much of this difference without identifying its historical cause.\\
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} historical isiXhosa; archival representation; sampling; measurement validity.

\section{Introduction}
\emph{Imvo Zabantsundu} was African-owned and published political debate outside missionary control. Mission-founded \emph{Isigidimi sama-Xosa} also carried African editors' and readers' writing, but differed in publishing history and schedule \citep{schoots2023archive}. As records of African intellectual life, these papers matter culturally; their surviving text is not a random sample of historical people.

In the overlapping 1885, 1887 and 1888 material, English-assigned text occupies 22.64\% of the average \emph{Imvo} page but only 1.40\% of an average \emph{Isigidimi} page; pooling eligible words yields 29.47\% and 1.28\%, respectively. \emph{Imvo} also printed English editorial translations \citep{schoots2023archive}. Thus weighting changes which text receives influence, not which historical opinions or speakers are represented. The labels denote archival text views, not adjudicated language IDs.

Historical newspaper work questions archival coverage and reweighting \citep{beelen2025,jo2020}, while data statements document selection \citep{bender2018}. Tokenizer work distinguishes unequal segmentation costs from predictive quality \citep{petrov2023,ali2024}. Unlike historically interpreted root-frequency studies \citep{schoots2023frequency}, our unannotated four-grams are not cultural concepts; we test measurement reliability before interpretation.

\noindent\textbf{Research questions.} \textbf{RQ1:} In \emph{Imvo}, do between-view allocation and within-view page choice have separable effects on segmentation and held-out bigram loss? \textbf{RQ2:} Across both papers, do 1885-to-1888 four-gram changes depend on weighting text positions, pages, issues or months, and survive issue-level calibration?

\section{Study design}
A page-versus-word tokenizer comparison changes both \emph{between-view allocation} and \emph{within-view page selection}. We cross page-like (P) and word-like (W) choices for both, producing PP, PW, WP and WW (allocation first). Page-like allocation averages page view shares; word-like allocation pools eligible words. Within-view selection samples pages by view share or view-word mass. The factorial contrasts locate the source of a sampling effect that PP--WW alone would conflate.

BPE and Unigram use matched budgets of about 500,000 alphabetic training characters and 2,000-piece vocabularies, with five seeds per policy and the same evaluation strings. Training uses \emph{Imvo} 1885 and January--September 1887; smoothing for count-bigram models is selected on October--December 1887, then 1888 is held out. The 1889--94 check is dependent because those years were inspected earlier. We measure segmentation in pieces per 100 characters and predictive loss in bits per character (BPC). Bigram models receive identical underlying training/test texts, so only the induced token sequences differ. Paired publication-issue bootstraps keep pages of the same issue together.

Independently, \emph{ArchiveLens} audits XH-assigned views in both newspapers. It fixes 120 within-word four-character sequences from 1887 before comparing 1885 with 1888, preventing direct selection on the reported year changes. Frequencies per 10,000 eligible positions either pool all positions (character mass) or average pages, issues or calendar months equally. These target, respectively, surviving text volume or a typical publication unit, not the same historical population. Let $\Delta_{g,w}$ be pattern $g$'s 1888-minus-1885 change under rule $w$; the maximum discrepancy from mass weighting is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gap}
 S_g=\max_{w\in\{\mathrm{page,issue,month}\}}
 |\Delta_{g,w}-\Delta_{g,\mathrm{mass}}|.
\end{equation}
Because signs can reverse near zero, we separately calibrate the overall reversal count and pattern-level maximum gaps against 4,999 whole-issue year-label permutations within calendar months. Holm correction covers 120 patterns per newspaper and test family \citep{holm1979}. Nested month/issue bootstrap intervals describe uncertainty; leave-one-issue and repeated-passage checks test sensitivity. Permutations are a conditional reference, not proof that historical years are exchangeable.

\section{Findings}
\textbf{RQ1.} Word-like view allocation reduces English BPE segmentation cost by 1.88 pieces per 100 characters but raises XH cost by 0.45; Unigram agrees in direction (Fig.~\ref{fig:models}a). In English BPE, allocation reduces count-bigram loss by 0.037 BPC while word-like \emph{page selection} raises it by 0.019 (Fig.~\ref{fig:models}b).

XH bigram effects are small and vary by seed; the later English WW--PP contrast is not robust to year resampling.

\textbf{RQ2.} \emph{Imvo}'s 23 sign reversals are unexceptional under permutation (mean 29, upper-tail $p=.888$). Yet 29 of 120 maximum estimator gaps survive Holm correction, each attained by equal-page versus character-mass weighting. None survive in \emph{Isigidimi}; its 12 issues per year versus \emph{Imvo}'s 49 and 52 preclude a stability conclusion.

For \emph{Imvo} \texttt{ants}, mass weighting yields $-0.23$ and equal-page weighting $+4.80$ per 10,000 positions (Fig.~\ref{fig:archive}b). Their maximum gap survives Holm adjustment ($p_{\mathrm{adj}}=.024$), not the mass-weighted temporal change. The bootstrap intervals are unadjusted; opposite point estimates are not two supported historical trends.

\section{Discussion}
The two studies isolate distinct threats to interpreting this archive. RQ1 distinguishes between-view exposure from page choice; in bilingual \emph{Imvo}, a pooled gain can hide a higher XH segmentation cost. Resegmentation changes count-bigram transitions, so fewer pieces need not lower predictive loss. We measure processing of surviving text, not fairness to historical speakers or neural-model performance.

RQ2 asks what a historical frequency describes. Character mass represents pooled text; equal pages, issues and months describe average publication units. For newspapers with different editorial histories and schedules, these need not answer the same question. Calibration distinguishes unexpectedly \emph{numerous} sign reversals (not found) from unusually \emph{large} estimator gaps (found). Neither licenses an interpretation of four-grams as political ideas.

IsiXIT retains the original page layout \citep{schoots2023archive}. For XH-assigned text, \emph{Imvo} second pages contain 4.24 (1885) and 3.69 (1888) times the first-page eligible positions; in \emph{Isigidimi}, the largest-to-smallest page-position averages are 1.30 and 1.42 (post-hoc checks). For page length $n_i$ and pattern rate $r_i$, $R_{\mathrm{mass}}-R_{\mathrm{page}}=\operatorname{Cov}(n_i,r_i)/\bar n$. Among the 29 corrected gaps, exploratory decomposition attributes a median 85\% of the discrepancy to \emph{between-page-position} variation. Excluding first pages reduces 25 gaps; masking exact repeats reduces 15 but enlarges 14. These diagnostics show archival structure, not an editorial cause: page positions were not linked to annotated genres.

For historical-language NLP, the defensible practice is to state the surviving sources, missing coverage and intended unit of evidence; separate sampling factors; and check alternative weights with issue-aware uncertainty before interpreting change. An average page is not a representative author or community. Limitations include archive-assigned language labels, repeated text, previously inspected years and the conditional year-permutation benchmark. The \href{https://github.com/Stralb/isixhosa-archive-nlp}{project repository} distinguishes frozen results from exploratory analysis.
